# Emergency Procedures for Yacht Captains: A Study Guide

This guide provides a quick-access, comprehensive resource for emergency procedures, formatted for study and practical application on a yacht in the Great Lakes. Each section includes **steps**, **rationale**, **regulatory references**, and **Great Lakes considerations**, ensuring you’re prepared for a captain’s exam and real-world scenarios.

## 1. Man Overboard (MOB)

### Quick-Access Steps
1. **Shout “Man Overboard!”** and note the side (port/starboard).
2. **Throw flotation devices** (life ring, cushion) toward the person.
3. **Mark position**:
   - Press GPS “MOB” button or record coordinates.
   - Deploy a dan buoy or floating marker.
4. **Assign a spotter** to maintain visual contact with the person.
5. **Execute recovery maneuver**:
   - **Williamson Turn** (for night/poor visibility): Turn 60° toward MOB side, then 240° in opposite direction to retrace path.
   - **Anderson Turn** (for small vessels, good visibility): Immediate hard turn toward MOB side, circle back.
   - **Reach Tack** (sailboats): Head into wind, tack, approach on close reach.
6. **Stop engines** when near the person.
7. **Recover** using a boarding ladder, rescue sling, or liferaft.
8. **Provide first aid** (e.g., treat hypothermia) and log the incident.

### Detailed Explanation
- **Rationale**: Immediate action is critical, as a person in cold Great Lakes water (e.g., 50°F in Lake Superior) can succumb to hypothermia within 30–60 minutes. The goal is to locate, approach, and recover the person safely while maintaining vessel control.
- **Procedure Details**:
  - **Shouting**: Alerts crew and initiates response. Specify side for clarity (e.g., “MOB starboard!”).
  - **Flotation**: Provides immediate buoyancy, increasing survival odds.
  - **Marking Position**: GPS coordinates or a buoy ensure you can return to the exact spot, crucial in choppy seas or low visibility.
  - **Spotter**: Continuous visual contact prevents losing sight of the person, especially in 3–6-foot waves common on Lake Michigan.
  - **Maneuvers**:
    - **Williamson Turn**: Ideal for retracing course in fog or darkness, ensuring the vessel returns to the MOB’s location.
    - **Anderson Turn**: Faster for power yachts (e.g., Tiara EX60), allowing quick circling in daylight.
    - **Reach Tack**: Used by sailboats to maintain control while positioning for recovery.
  - **Recovery**: Approach into the wind/current for stability. Use a rescue sling or liferaft for weak swimmers. Avoid propellers by stopping engines.
  - **First Aid**: Cold water immersion requires immediate warming (blankets, dry clothes) to prevent hypothermia.
- **Great Lakes Considerations**:
  - **Cold Water**: Lake temperatures rarely exceed 70°F, even in summer, necessitating rapid recovery and hypothermia protocols.
  - **Sudden Squalls**: Lake Huron’s fast-moving storms can obscure visibility, making GPS marking critical.
  - **Shallow Areas**: Near harbors (e.g., Lake Erie), avoid grounding during maneuvers.
- **Regulatory References**:
  - USCG Navigation Rules (Rule 8): Requires vessels to take immediate action to avoid collision or harm.
  - 46 CFR 185.520: Mandates crew training for MOB recovery on passenger vessels.
- **Study Tips**:
  - Memorize the Williamson Turn sequence (60° + 240°).
  - Practice plotting MOB coordinates on a chart.
  - Review USCG exam questions on MOB procedures (e.g., “What is the first action after an MOB alert?”).

## 2. Medical Emergencies

### Quick-Access Steps
1. **Assess the situation**:
   - Ensure scene safety (e.g., secure vessel, avoid hazards).
   - Check victim’s responsiveness (AVPU scale: Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive).
2. **Provide first aid**:
   - **CPR**: If no pulse/breathing, start at 100–120 compressions/min.
   - **Bleeding**: Apply direct pressure, elevate, use tourniquet if severe.
   - **Shock**: Lay flat, elevate legs, keep warm.
3. **Activate emergency response**:
   - Contact USCG via VHF Channel 16 or 911 if near shore.
   - Provide location, nature of injury, and number of victims.
4. **Use onboard medical kit**:
   - Administer oxygen, AED, or medications (e.g., epinephrine for anaphylaxis) as trained.
5. **Prepare for evacuation**:
   - Clear deck for helicopter or rescue boat.
   - Document vitals and treatment for responders.
6. **Log incident** in the vessel’s logbook.

### Detailed Explanation
- **Rationale**: Medical emergencies on a yacht require rapid assessment and stabilization, as professional help may be hours away on the Great Lakes (e.g., 50 miles offshore in Lake Superior). Captains must act as first responders until rescue arrives.
- **Procedure Details**:
  - **Assessment**: Use AVPU to determine consciousness. Check ABCs (Airway, Breathing, Circulation) to prioritize treatment.
  - **First Aid**:
    - **CPR**: Follow American Heart Association guidelines (2-inch compressions, 30:2 ratio with rescue breaths).
    - **Bleeding**: Severe arterial bleeding (e.g., from a propeller injury) requires a tourniquet, applied 2–3 inches above the wound.
    - **Shock**: Common in trauma; elevating legs increases blood flow to vital organs.
  - **Emergency Response**: VHF Channel 16 is the primary distress channel, monitored by USCG stations (e.g., Sector Sault Ste. Marie). Provide concise details: “Mayday, this is [vessel name], heart attack, 45°12.3’N, 087°15.6’W, one victim, request medevac.”
  - **Medical Kit**: USCG requires vessels over 39.4 feet to carry a first aid kit (46 CFR 184.710). Kits should include bandages, AED, oxygen, and trauma supplies.
  - **Evacuation**: Helicopter medevacs are common on the Great Lakes; ensure a clear landing area and secure loose gear.
  - **Documentation**: Log time, actions, and victim status for legal and medical handoff.
- **Great Lakes Considerations**:
  - **Remote Areas**: Lake Superior’s north shore has limited cell coverage, relying on VHF or satellite phones.
  - **Cold Exposure**: Hypothermia can exacerbate medical conditions, requiring warming measures.
  - **USCG Response**: Stations like Duluth or Cleveland provide rapid response, but transit times vary (e.g., 30–60 minutes).
- **Regulatory References**:
  - 46 CFR 184.710: Mandates first aid equipment and training.
  - SOLAS Chapter III: Requires life-saving appliances and medical preparedness.
- **Study Tips**:
  - Memorize CPR ratios and tourniquet application.
  - Practice a mock VHF distress call for a heart attack scenario.
  - Review USCG first aid requirements (e.g., “What must a medical kit include?”).

## 3. Abandoning Ship

### Quick-Access Steps
1. **Issue abandon ship order**:
   - Sound six short blasts followed by one long blast on the horn.
   - Announce via PA: “Prepare to abandon ship.”
2. **Don life jackets** and immersion suits (if applicable).
3. **Gather survival gear**:
   - EPIRB, PLBs, VHF radio, flares, water, food, first aid kit.
4. **Launch liferaft**:
   - Secure painter line, inflate raft, board from leeward side.
5. **Activate EPIRB** and deploy PLBs.
6. **Abandon vessel**:
   - Enter liferaft, cut painter line, paddle away to avoid sinking vessel.
7. **Maintain group cohesion** and await rescue.
8. **Log decision** in logbook (if possible).

### Detailed Explanation
- **Rationale**: Abandoning ship is a last resort when the vessel is sinking, on fire, or otherwise untenable. The goal is to ensure crew/passenger safety in a liferaft until rescue.
- **Procedure Details**:
  - **Order**: Clear communication prevents panic. The horn signal (6 short + 1 long) is a universal distress alert.
  - **Life Jackets/Suits**: USCG-approved Type I PFDs are mandatory (46 CFR 180.71). Immersion suits are critical in the Great Lakes’ cold water (e.g., 40°F in spring).
  - **Survival Gear**: EPIRBs transmit a 406 MHz signal to SAR satellites, pinpointing location. PLBs provide individual tracking. Pack a grab bag with essentials (water: 0.5L/person/day; flares: 6 parachute, 6 handheld).
  - **Liferaft**: Modern liferafts (e.g., Revere Coastal Elite) are SOLAS-compliant, with canopies and ballast pockets for stability. Board from the leeward side to avoid capsizing.
  - **EPIRB/PLB**: Activation sends a distress signal to USCG via COSPAS-SARSAT, typically resulting in a response within 1–2 hours.
  - **Abandonment**: Paddle 100–200 yards to avoid suction from a sinking vessel. Stay together to maximize visibility.
- **Great Lakes Considerations**:
  - **Cold Water**: Survival time in 50°F water is 1–2 hours without immersion suits.
  - **Shipping Lanes**: Lakes Michigan and Huron have busy freighter traffic, increasing rescue chances but requiring vigilance.
  - **Weather**: Sudden storms (e.g., 40-knot winds on Lake Erie) can complicate liferaft deployment.
- **Regulatory References**:
  - 46 CFR 180.68: Requires liferafts and EPIRBs for vessels over 65 feet.
  - SOLAS Chapter IV: Mandates distress signaling equipment.
- **Study Tips**:
  - Memorize the abandon ship signal (6 short + 1 long).
  - Practice EPIRB activation steps.
  - Review USCG exam scenarios (e.g., “When should you abandon ship?”).

## 5. Storm Tactics

### Quick-Access Steps
1. **Monitor weather**:
   - Check NOAA Weather Radio (162.400–162.550 MHz) or Navtex.

2. **Prepare vessel**:

   - Check bilge pumps, fuel levels, and battery charge.
3. **Reduce sail (sailboats)** or throttle down (powerboats).
4. **Choose tactic**:
   - **Run with the storm**: Steer downwind at 5–7 knots, trailing warps/drogue.
   - **Heave-to**: Position boat at 45° to waves, minimizing motion (sailboats).
   - **Sea anchor**: Deploy from bow to keep head to waves (last resort).
5. **Navigate safely**:
   - Avoid lee shores (e.g., Lake Michigan’s eastern coast).
   - Maintain 500-meter separation from other vessels.
6. **Communicate**:
   - Broadcast “Securité” on VHF Channel 16 for storm updates.
   - Notify USCG of position if conditions worsen.
7. **Log actions** and monitor crew safety.

### Detailed Explanation
- **Rationale**: Great Lakes storms (e.g., 50-knot gales on Lake Huron) can arise within 30 minutes, requiring proactive tactics to protect the vessel and crew.
- **Procedure Details**:
  - **Weather Monitoring**: NOAA’s continuous broadcasts provide storm warnings (e.g., “Small Craft Advisory: 25-knot winds”). A falling barometer (>1 mb/hour) signals approaching lows.
  - **Preparation**: Secured gear prevents injuries; life jackets are mandatory in 6+ foot seas. Functional bilge pumps are critical for flooding risks.
  - **Tactics**:
    - **Running**: Maintains control in following seas, using a drogue (e.g., parachute anchor) to slow speed and prevent broaching.
    - **Heaving-to**: Creates a slick to windward, reducing wave impact (ideal for sailboats like a Sabre 38).
    - **Sea Anchor**: Keeps the bow into waves, used when space is limited or crew is fatigued.
  - **Navigation**: Avoid shallow areas (e.g., Lake Erie’s 20-foot depths) where waves steepen. Use radar to track nearby vessels.
  - **Communication**: “Securité” alerts others to hazards (e.g., “Securité, Tiara One, 44°30’N, 087°00’W, experiencing 40-knot winds”).
- **Great Lakes Considerations**:
  - **Sudden Storms**: Lake Michigan’s “witch of November” storms (e.g., 1975 Edmund Fitzgerald sinking) highlight the need for vigilance.
  - **Short Waves**: Steep, short-period waves (4–6 seconds) require robust hulls (e.g., Tiara’s deep-V).
