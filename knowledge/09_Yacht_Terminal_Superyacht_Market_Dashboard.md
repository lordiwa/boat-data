### 1. Historical Sales Data & Transaction Records
This is the biggest gap in free access. Most sites track **recent/confirmed brokerage sales** (asking vs. sold prices are rare publicly), but historical depth varies:

- **BOATPro (BOAT International)**: Strongest overall for superyachts (24m+). Access to sales from ~2010 onward (thousands of records), filterable by builder, length, name, month, asking price. Daily updates on new sales, price changes, central agency shifts. Includes 8–10+ years of brokerage history for trends. Overlaps with others on recent deals but adds verified data and analytics.
- **YachtBuyer MarketWatch**: Claims one of the most comprehensive databases—tracks 16,000+ yachts (>24m), 15+ years of real market data, verified build records, cross-checked listings to eliminate duplicates. Focuses on intelligence for brokers (sales volume, authenticity).
- **VesselsValue**: Tracks values for 6,000+ megayachts (78ft+/24m+), with **historical values back to 2012**, purchase transactions, and new-build contracts. One of the few making some transaction data more accessible (previously insider-only).
- **Yachtsnl**: Niche but useful for Dutch-built yachts—purchase sales history showing time on market, first/last asking prices, listing broker(s), and selling broker.
- **Broker-specific (e.g., Northrop & Johnson, IYC, Denison, Fraser)**: Public "sales records" or "recent sales" pages list their deals (often with values or ranges for big ones). Strong overlap on high-profile transactions, but siloed.

**Overlaps**: All major players (BOATPro, SuperYacht Times, broker sites) report the same confirmed 2025 sales (~400–600+ brokerage deals, €7+ billion range). Differences come in verification methods and how far back they go. Off-market/private sales are often rumored but not fully priced.

**What’s missing for a full dataset**: Complete sold prices (many are confidential), full chain of ownership/sales history per yacht, and pre-2010 data. You'd need to combine subscriptions + manual broker outreach or paid one-off reports.

### 2. Yacht Owner History & Current Ownership

Includes ownership shifts, nationality breakdowns (e.g., US leading, rising Asia), and "who owns what" insights. Live AIS tracking + historical movement. Often called the "yachting Bloomberg." Owner details in subscriber version.
- **SuperYachtFan**: Sells Excel databases (e.g., 1,680 yachts + 760 billionaires/owners for ~€145, with updates). Lists current owners, some history, and cross-references with jets.
- **BOATPro**: Fleet tracking with AIS (historical 5+ years), but owner names often anonymized or via intelligence reports.
- **VesselsValue & others**: Some ownership links via value databases.

**Overlaps & Gaps**: Nationality/aggregate stats overlap heavily (e.g., top owning countries). Individual owner history is pieced together from news, refit announcements, or sales reports. Serial owners (who flip yachts) are trackable via sales data, but corporate/flag structures obscure true beneficial owners. No public "complete chain" exists for most vessels.

### 3. Vintage / Classic Yachts Area (Pre-2000 or Heritage Builds)
This is a distinct, less data-rich segment—more passion-driven, lower volumes, higher restoration focus.

- Dedicated brokers and sites (e.g., Classic Boat magazine insights, specialized classic yacht brokers) track sales in 30–60ft+ classic motor/sailing yachts.
- Market trends: Growing interest from new buyers (30–50% newcomers in some reports); prices often fraction of new builds. Classic motor yachts market projected to grow (e.g., ~USD 2.5B to 4.2B+ by 2030 in some estimates).
- Data availability: Sparse compared to modern superyachts. Sales via niche listings; refit stories common. Overlaps minimally with superyacht databases unless a classic is 24m+.

**Key addition for your terminal**: Separate "Vintage Watchlist" with build year filters, restoration costs, and heritage builder output (e.g., older Feadship, Lürssen classics).

### 4. Refit, Service & Maintenance Data
Often overlooked but huge—refit market value has at times exceeded new-builds in spend.

- **The Superyacht Report / SuperYacht Times**: Dedicated refit reports, statistics on refit activity, yards involved.
- **Market reports**: Global yacht refit/maintenance ~USD 2.5–6.4B (2023–2025), growing 5.8–8% CAGR to 2032–2035. Superyachts drive ~39% share due to tech upgrades, sustainability retrofits, and longevity focus.
- **Yard-specific**: Service histories via shipyard records or management software (not public). Refit announcements often tied to sales (e.g., "refit 2024/2025" boosts value).
- **Overlaps**: Broker listings note recent refits; BOATPro/SYT track activity indirectly via fleet movements or new-build vs. brokerage trends.

**What’s missing**: Granular per-yacht service logs, cost data, or predictive maintenance analytics (available only to owners/managers via private software like YachtWave, Seahub, etc.).

### Yacht Racing

#### History

'''The History of Yachting''' traces the transformation of fast, shallow-draft vessels from practical naval tools into symbols of wealth, adventure, competition, and ultimate luxury. What began as Dutch "jachtschips" (hunt ships) in the 14th–17th centuries for chasing pirates evolved into the superyacht lifestyle of the 21st century — floating palaces with helipads, submarines, hybrid propulsion, and explorer capabilities.

==== Origins (14th–17th Centuries) ====
The term "yacht" derives from the Dutch ''jacht'', referring to swift vessels used by the Dutch Navy to pursue smugglers and pirates in coastal waters. In 1660, the Dutch gifted the 20-metre ''Mary'' to King Charles II of England upon his restoration. Charles and his brother raced yachts on the River Thames (first recorded wager in 1662), igniting aristocratic interest in Britain. Early yachts blended sailing performance with emerging comfort.

==== 18th–19th Centuries: Racing & Clubs ====
Yacht clubs formalized the sport. The Water Club of Cork (Ireland, 1720; later Royal Cork Yacht Club) is among the oldest. Britain's Royal Yacht Squadron formed in 1815 at Cowes. The New York Yacht Club (1844) elevated American participation. The 1851 America's Cup — won by the schooner ''America'' against British challengers — became yachting's most famous trophy, still contested today.

Steam power arrived mid-19th century, enabling larger, more comfortable motor yachts. The "Golden Age of Yachting" (late 19th–early 20th century) saw industrial magnates commission opulent vessels. J.P. Morgan's series of ''Corsair'' yachts set standards for luxury.

==== 20th Century: Motorization & Opulence ====
Internal combustion engines and diesel revolutionized yachting, shifting from sail-dominant to motor yachts. Aristotle Onassis's ''Christina O'' (converted frigate) and vessels like ''Sea Cloud'' exemplified floating mansions with cinemas, pools, and libraries. Post-WWII economic booms and technological advances (aluminium hulls, advanced navigation) expanded access among the ultra-wealthy. The superyacht era emerged in the 1980s–1990s with vessels over 30–40m becoming common.

==== 21st Century: Superyacht & Megayacht Boom ====
Lengths exploded: from 100m+ "gigayachts" like ''Azzam'' (180.6m, Lürssen, 2013) to explorer vessels and hybrid/sustainable designs. Key drivers include UHNW wealth, carbon-fiber composites, beach clubs, wellness suites, and support vessels (e.g., Jeff Bezos's support ship for ''Koru''). Sustainability, AI systems, and adventure (expedition yachts) define the current chapter. Racing circuits (St Barths Bucket, Maxi Rolex Cup) and boat shows remain social anchors.

==== Impact on Culture & Economy ====
Yachting evolved from royal pastime to global industry valued in billions, influencing design, shipbuilding, and lifestyle. Famous transitions include wooden hulls → steel/aluminium → composites; sail → steam/diesel → hybrid/fuel-cell.

### Crew Training Standards (Wikipedia-Style Section)

**Crew training standards** in the superyacht industry ensure safety, professionalism, and compliance with international maritime regulations. Unlike commercial shipping (cargo, tankers, or large cruise ships), superyacht training balances rigorous safety requirements with high-end service, guest hospitality, and the unique operational demands of vessels ranging from 24m to 180m+.

The foundation is the **International Convention on Standards of Training, Certification and Watchkeeping for Seafarers (STCW)**, as amended (commonly called STCW '95 or STCW 2010 Manila Amendments). Additional certifications, medical fitness, and role-specific training are required depending on the yacht's flag state, size, commercial vs. private use, and whether it carries fare-paying guests (charter operations).

#### Core Mandatory Requirements (Entry-Level for Most Superyacht Crew)
All crew members (deck, interior/stewardess, engineer, chef, etc.) typically need the following before joining a professional yacht, especially those over 24m or operating commercially:

1. **STCW Basic Safety Training (BST)** — The universal minimum.
   This 5-day package (sometimes offered as separate modules) includes four core components:
   - **Personal Survival Techniques (PST)**: Sea survival, life rafts, abandonment procedures (1 day).
   - **Fire Prevention and Fire Fighting (FPFF)**: Fire theory, extinguishers, breathing apparatus (2 days).
   - **Elementary First Aid (EFA)**: Basic medical response at sea.
   - **Personal Safety and Social Responsibility (PSSR)**: Workplace safety, team dynamics, pollution prevention.
   Many yachts also require **Proficiency in Security Awareness** (or Designated Security Duties for ISPS Code vessels).
   **Validity**: Certificates generally require refresher training every 5 years.
   **Why mandatory**: Required by SOLAS, MLC 2006, and most flag states/insurers for commercial or large yachts. Private yachts often expect it anyway for insurance and best practice.

2. **ENG1 Medical Certificate (or equivalent seafarer medical)**
   - Issued by MCA-approved doctors (UK standard, widely recognized internationally).
   - Involves full medical history review, vision/hearing tests, physical exam, and fitness assessment for sea duties.
   - **Validity**: Usually 2 years (shorter in some cases).
   - Alternatives: Some countries' equivalents are accepted by the MCA/flag states, but ENG1 remains the "gold standard" for superyacht careers. USCG medicals are not direct equivalents for UK-flagged or many international yachts.
   - Purpose: Ensures crew are medically fit and won't endanger themselves or others at sea.

Additional common entry requirements:
- Valid passport.
- Sea time / experience logs (e.g., for Yacht Rating Certificate: minimum 6 months onboard including 2 months seagoing on vessels ≥15m).
- Background checks and sometimes drug testing.

#### Role-Specific and Advanced Training

- **Security**: Proficiency in Designated Security Duties (PDSD) for ISPS-compliant vessels.

'''AIS Tracking Technologies''' (Automatic Identification System) is an automated VHF-based tracking system that allows vessels to exchange real-time data including identity, position, course, speed, and voyage information. Originally developed for collision avoidance, AIS has become essential for superyacht fleet management, brokerage intelligence, charter operations, security, and regulatory compliance.

In the superyacht world, dedicated platforms like **BOATPro Fleet Tracker** and **SuperYacht Times iQ** provide superyacht-specific AIS solutions that filter out commercial shipping noise and focus on vessels ≥24m.

See also: [[Yacht Anatomy]] (integration with bridge and propulsion), [[Propulsion Systems]], [[Yacht Maintenance and Refit]] (system upkeep), [[Captain Certification and Career Pathways]] (training on AIS use).

== How AIS Works ==
AIS transponders on vessels broadcast data over two dedicated VHF frequencies (161.975 MHz and 162.025 MHz) using Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA). Each transmission includes:
- **Static data**: MMSI number, vessel name, call sign, dimensions, type.
- **Dynamic data**: GPS-derived position, course over ground (COG), speed over ground (SOG), heading.
- **Voyage data**: Destination, ETA, draught, cargo/passenger details (where applicable).

Data is received by:
- Nearby vessels (line-of-sight, typically 10–40 nautical miles).
- Shore-based terrestrial receivers (coastal networks).
- Satellites (Satellite AIS or S-AIS) for global/open-ocean coverage.

**Class A AIS** (mandatory for ships ≥300 GT and all passenger vessels): High-power (12.5W), frequent updates (every 2–10 seconds when underway).
**Class B AIS** (common on yachts): Lower power (2–5W), less frequent updates; suitable for most superyachts.

== Terrestrial vs. Satellite AIS ==
- **Terrestrial AIS**: Shore-based receivers provide high-accuracy, near real-time data in coastal waters and busy areas. Coverage is excellent near ports but limited offshore (typically <50 nautical miles).
- **Satellite AIS (S-AIS)**: Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellites capture signals in remote oceans. Coverage is global but can have latency (minutes to hours) and gaps for low-power Class B transmissions. Providers combine both for hybrid tracking.

**2026 Trends**:
- Hybrid systems (terrestrial + satellite) deliver near-global coverage.
- AI-enhanced platforms detect anomalies, predict movements, or flag "dark ships" (AIS switched off).
- Superyacht-specific tools (e.g., BOATPro) filter only verified yachts ≥24m and provide 5+ years of historical data.

== AIS in the Superyacht Industry ==
Major platforms tailored for superyachts:
- **BOATPro Fleet Tracker**: Dedicated superyacht AIS with terrestrial + satellite coverage, historical tracking (5+ years), custom mapping, port/marina reporting, and verified fleet counts. It excludes commercial vessels for clean superyacht intelligence.
