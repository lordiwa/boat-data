## Grok

**Shipwreck treasure diving in Florida, particularly along the Treasure Coast and Florida Keys, has a rich history tied to Spanish colonial fleets lost in hurricanes.** Major wrecks include the 1622 *Nuestra Señora de Atocha* and sister ships, the 1715 Treasure Fleet (Plate Fleet), and the 1733 fleet. These carried vast quantities of gold, silver coins (pieces of eight/reales), escudos, ingots, emeralds, and artifacts. Modern salvage began with hard-hat and early scuba divers, evolving into a mix of professional operations and recreational diving.

### Early Diving and US Navy Involvement
US Navy diving in Florida waters historically focused on salvage, rescue, and military operations rather than treasure hunting. Navy divers used hard-hat equipment and, later, rebreathers or early scuba for tasks like harbor clearance, wreck surveys, and training. During and after WW2, Navy Underwater Demolition Teams (UDT, precursors to SEALs) trained in Florida waters, contributing to practical diving knowledge.

Navy involvement in historic shipwrecks was limited but included surveys or incidental finds. Post-WW2, the Navy sank vessels as artificial reefs or for training (e.g., various YDT dive tenders and other warships in the Panhandle and Keys). Recreational wreck diving later boomed on these and natural wrecks. Early Navy salvage efforts on older wrecks were not primarily treasure-oriented but helped map sites.

### Pioneers of Recreational/Commercial Treasure Diving (Pre- and Post-WW2 into the 1960s)
**Art McKee** (1910–1979), often called the "father of modern treasure hunting," was a key pioneer in the Florida Keys. A hard-hat diver who arrived in Florida in the 1930s, he salvaged wrecks like those from the 1733 fleet (e.g., *San Pedro* or *Capitana*). He recovered significant silver bars (earning the nickname "Silver Bar" McKee) and opened the Museum of Sunken Treasure. McKee influenced popular culture through TV shows like *Danger Is My Business* and helped develop tools like early underwater metal detectors. His work predates widespread scuba and bridged helmet diving to modern salvage.

Recreational scuba took off after Jacques Cousteau and Émile Gagnan's Aqua-Lung (1943). Post-WW2, Florida saw clubs form (e.g., Florida Skin Divers Association in 1952). The warm, clear waters and accessible wrecks made it ideal for sport diving, spearfishing, and early wreck exploration in the 1950s.

**Kip Wagner** (1906–1972) is credited with sparking the modern treasure rush on the Treasure Coast. In the late 1950s, while beachcombing near Sebastian after storms, he found a Spanish silver coin (piece of eight) from the 1715 fleet. This led to systematic searches. On January 8, 1961, his group recovered thousands of silver coins in clumps from a wreck site (often called the "Cabin Wreck"). They formed the Real Eight Company (incorporating eight members) in 1961, recovering millions in gold and silver from 1715 sites. Wagner's 1965 *National Geographic* article ("Drowned Galleons Yield Spanish Gold") brought worldwide attention.

**Mel Fisher** (1922–1998), a former chicken farmer and diving shop owner (opened one of California's first), moved to Florida and joined forces with Wagner's circle. His company, Treasure Salvors, Inc., famously pursued the 1622 *Atocha*. After 16 years and significant investment (and tragedy, including family losses), they located the "Mother Lode" in 1985, recovering an estimated $450+ million in gold, silver, emeralds, and artifacts (including tons of silver and thousands of coins). Fisher fought legal battles with the State of Florida over ownership (ultimately winning major rights via Supreme Court). His work popularized treasure diving globally.

These pioneers operated under state leases (typically 25% to Florida) in the 1960s, blending hard-hat, scuba, and later magnetometer/airlift technology. Their efforts turned the "Treasure Coast" (around Vero Beach/Sebastian) into a legendary area.

### First Diver(s) to Recover "Real Gold"
The earliest modern recoveries of gold from these wrecks are tied to the 1715 fleet pioneers. Kip Wagner's group found silver first (1959–1961), but gold coins and artifacts followed as they expanded operations. Specific "first gold" attributions are less pinpointed in popular accounts than the initial silver finds, as early recoveries included mixed cargoes. Wagner's team and Real Eight recovered thousands of gold escudos alongside silver.

McKee recovered silver bars prominently earlier, but gold finds in the Keys (1733/others) were also significant. No single "first diver" for gold is universally documented as a eureka moment like Wagner's silver coin; recoveries were team efforts with incremental finds. Later notable individual gold recoveries include various 1715 fleet divers (e.g., modern ones like Eric Schmitt or Levin Shavers finding gold escudos/coins in recent decades).

### Other Areas with Gold from Shipwrecks
- **Florida Keys**: 1622 fleet (*Atocha*, *Santa Margarita*) — primary gold/silver/emerald source; 1733 fleet wrecks also yielded treasure.
- **Treasure Coast (East Coast, Sebastian to Fort Pierce)**: 1715 fleet — massive ongoing recoveries of gold coins, chains, jewelry. Beach finds still occur after storms.
- Scattered other Spanish wrecks or colonial vessels along Florida coasts have produced smaller gold finds, but the major fleets dominate.
- Beyond Florida: Famous gold shipwrecks elsewhere include the SS *Central America* (California Gold Rush, off the US East Coast) and various Caribbean/Atlantic Spanish galleons, but Florida's are the most dived and salvaged in US waters.

### Evolution into the 1960s and Beyond
The 1960s marked the boom: scuba accessibility, magazines, and media (National Geographic, TV) fueled recreational diving clubs and tourism. Professional salvors used boats with crews for magnetometer surveys, excavation, and artifact recovery. State regulations increased, balancing salvage with archaeology. By the late 1960s–1970s, operations like Fisher's were full-time professional endeavors, while recreational divers explored wrecks for sport.

Today, companies like Mel Fisher's Treasures and 1715 Fleet–Queens Jewels continue operations under permits, with finds still making headlines (e.g., 2025 recoveries of 1,000+ coins). Recreational wreck diving thrives on artificial and historic reefs.

This history blends adventure, legal drama, technological innovation, and maritime archaeology. For deeper reading, sources include Wikipedia entries on Mel Fisher/Kip Wagner, *Pieces of Eight* by Wagner, and museums like Mel Fisher's in Sebastian or the History of Diving Museum in the Keys.

**July 20, 1985: The Discovery of the Atocha Mother Lode**

On **July 20, 1985**, after more than 16 years of systematic searching, the salvage team of **Mel Fisher** and Treasure Salvors, Inc. located the primary "Mother Lode" (main cargo pile) of the Spanish galleon *Nuestra Señora de Atocha*, which had sunk in 1622. This breakthrough is widely regarded as one of the most significant underwater treasure discoveries in history.

### The Famous Radio Message
**Kane Fisher** (Mel's son and captain of the salvage vessel *Dauntless*) radioed back to company headquarters in Key West with the jubilant message:

> **"Put away the charts; we've found the main pile!"** (or variations: "We've got the Mother Lode!").

This call confirmed the find of a concentrated deposit of treasure on the seafloor. The phrase became legendary in treasure-hunting lore, symbolizing the end of a long, expensive, and often discouraging quest. Mel Fisher was not aboard the *Dauntless* that day but received the news at headquarters.

### Timeline of the Discovery Day
- The *Dauntless* was anchored in approximately **55 feet (17 m)** of water near the **Marquesas Keys**, about 35–40 miles west of Key West in the Florida Straits (Hawks Channel area).
- Divers were in the water early (before 8 a.m.).
- **Previous day (July 19)**: Kane Fisher’s team found promising signs, including thousands of ballast stones in a sand hole. They stayed anchored overnight.
- **Key divers**:
  - **Andy Matroci** (first mate on the *Dauntless*) swam out and made the initial major contact. He surfaced excited, reporting they were "sitting on silver bars."
  - **Greg Wareham** (26-year-old former dive instructor) also hit strong metal detector readings and helped confirm the pile.
- The site appeared as a "reef of silver bars" with chests of coins, silver plates, ingots, copper items, ballast stones, and artifacts strewn across the bottom.

### Immediate Finds and Confirmation
- Initial haul included **1,041 silver bars/ingots** and multiple boxes of coins (thousands per box).
- Shipper’s marks on the bars quickly matched entries in the *Atocha*’s original cargo manifest from Spanish archives (Archive of the Indies in Seville), confirming the identification.
- The find included silver coins ("pieces of eight"), gold items, emeralds, and other artifacts. Excavation of the Mother Lode began immediately using prop-wash "mailboxes," airlifts, and metal detectors.

- The team had previously recovered confirming artifacts: silver bars in 1973 and five bronze cannons in 1975 (inscribed with *Atocha* markings).
- The search used historical research, magnetometers, side-scan sonar, and persistence across a vast, shifting sandy seabed.
- Exactly 10 years earlier (July 20, 1975), a tragic accident occurred: Mel’s oldest son **Dirk Fisher**, his wife **Angel**, and diver **Rick Gage** died when the salvage boat *Northwind* capsized. The 1985 discovery fell on the anniversary, adding emotional weight.

### Aftermath and Impact
- The discovery sparked massive media attention. Mel Fisher appeared on *The Tonight Show* with Johnny Carson on July 30, 1985.
- Celebrations included crew parties; singer **Jimmy Buffett** reportedly visited and sang atop silver bars.
- Total recovered value from the *Atocha* (and related *Santa Margarita*) is estimated at **~$450 million** (1980s dollars), including over 40 tons of silver/gold, hundreds of thousands of coins, thousands of emeralds, and artifacts.

This event transformed Mel Fisher from a persistent salvor into a global icon of treasure hunting. The motto **"Today's the Day!"** was finally realized after 16+ years of investment, technological innovation, family tragedy, and relentless effort in Florida waters. Artifacts are displayed at the Mel Fisher Maritime Heritage Museums in Key West and Sebastian.

### Edmond Halley's Diving Bell (1690–1691)
English astronomer, mathematician, and polymath (1656–1742), famous for Halley's Comet. He developed practical diving technology in the late 1680s–early 1690s and personally tested it.

**Design and Specifications** (from Halley's own 1691 paper to the Royal Society):
- **Shape and Size**: Truncated cone (wooden "diving tub"), ~**5 feet (1.52 m) diameter at the open bottom**, **3 feet (0.91 m) at the top**, **~5 feet deep** (~60 cubic feet volume). Large enough for 2–4 men.

This countered rising water pressure and allowed extended time underwater.
- **Additional Features**: Bench inside for resting. Experimental helmet-like smaller bell for mobility outside the main bell (dangerous; use unclear).

**Testing and Use**:
- Halley tested it personally, descending to ~60 feet (18 m) and staying for extended periods (hours in some accounts).
- Used for salvage (e.g., a sunken ship) and scientific observation. Allowed divers to exit the bell for work and return.
- Effective at 20–60 feet depths. Influential for decades and a major step toward modern diving.
