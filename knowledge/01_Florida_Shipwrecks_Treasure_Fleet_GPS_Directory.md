### Spanish Salvage (1715–Early 1700s)
Immediately after the disaster, Spanish authorities organized recovery from Havana and St. Augustine. Survivors set up beach camps; salvage sloops and crews used:

- **Dragging and grappling**: Hooks, nets, or drags to pull up wreckage, chests, and loose cargo from shallow waters.
- **Diving bells and free divers**: Enslaved or experienced divers (including pearl fishermen) worked in limited depths for coins, bullion, and artifacts. Operations lasted months to years, recovering significant portions (e.g., millions in value by late 1715) before sand buried remains.
- Challenges: Hurricanes, indigenous resistance, pirates, disease. Camps left archaeological traces.

Much cargo was recovered, but scattered debris and deeper/buried items remained for later salvors.

### Pioneering Modern Salvage (1950s–1970s): Kip Wagner & Real Eight Company
Kip Wagner discovered coins on beaches post-storm in the late 1950s. He formed the Real Eight Company (1961) with partners including Harry Cannon and Lou Ullian.

- **Initial methods**: Beach metal detecting (war-surplus mine detectors), shoveling/screening at Spanish camp sites, swimming/snorkeling near reefs for cannons/anchors.
- **Boat-based**: Small vessels; early dragging or basic dredging.
- **Key innovation collaboration**: Mel Fisher joined, contributing the **"mailbox" or prop-wash deflection system** (large tubes over boat propellers). Anchored boat engines redirect clear surface water downward, excavating sand in a controlled "hole" or crater for visibility and artifact exposure. This was far more effective than prior dredging and enabled the famous 1964 "carpet of gold" find (over 1,000 gold coins at Douglas Beach).
- Magnetometers for detecting ferrous metals (cannons, anchors, ballast) and basic underwater work.

They recovered millions in gold/silver coins, jewelry, and artifacts, documented in Wagner’s *Pieces of Eight* and National Geographic.

### Mel Fisher & Treasure Salvors Era
Fisher refined techniques on 1715 sites before his famous *Atocha* search. Mailboxes remained central; teams used side-scan sonar, better magnetometers, and systematic grid searches. Operations continued sporadically into the 1980s, with state splits (typically 20% to Florida).

**Prop-wash deflection (also called "mailboxes," "prop-wash deflectors," or "blowers")** is a key technique in shallow-water shipwreck salvage, particularly on sandy seabeds like those of Florida's Treasure Coast (1715 Fleet sites). It was popularized and refined by Mel Fisher and his teams in the 1960s.

### Basic Mechanics and Setup
The system redirects the high-velocity water flow (prop wash) from a boat's propeller(s) downward to the seabed:

1. **The "Mailbox" Device**:
   - Large, hinged or pivoting metal tubes, ducts, or deflectors (often cylindrical or box-like, resembling a mailbox) mounted at the stern of the salvage vessel.
   - They are lowered/positioned directly over or behind the propeller(s) when the boat is at the site.
   - Modern versions on boats like the *Magruder* or *Dare* can operate independently for better control and are engineered with hinges for raising/lowering.

2. **Operation**:
   - The boat is securely anchored (usually with multiple anchors to hold position against the thrust).
   - Engines are engaged in forward gear (at controlled RPMs).
   - Propellers accelerate water backward. The deflectors redirect this powerful horizontal flow **vertically downward** toward the seafloor.

3. **Physical Effects on the Seabed**:
   - **Clear Water Column**: Surface water is typically clearer than the turbid bottom layer. The downward jet brings this clear water to the divers, improving visibility dramatically.
   - **Excavation/Sand Removal**: The high-velocity water jet acts like a powerful blower or dredge. It scours and displaces sand, silt, or overburden (often several feet deep), creating a crater or hole. This exposes buried artifacts, coins, cannons, ballast, and ship structure without constant manual digging.
   - The force is strong enough to "blow" a wide hole quickly — in one famous 1964 test on a 1715 site, it revealed over 1,000 gold coins.

**Fluid Dynamics Principle**: Propellers generate thrust by accelerating a mass of water rearward (Newton's third law: action-reaction). The deflector changes the direction of this momentum transfer, converting horizontal prop wash into a focused downward plume. Efficiency depends on tube shape, clearance around the prop, and RPM. Too tight a fit can create backpressure; designs balance flow acceleration with minimal loss.

### Advantages in Treasure Salvage
- **Efficient for Sandy Sites**: Ideal for the dynamic, shifting sands covering 1715 Fleet wrecks (10–50+ ft depths).
- **Combines Search & Recovery**: Divers work in/around the excavation zone using metal detectors, hand-fanning, or small suction tools for delicate artifacts.
- **Scalable**: Twin engines often have independent mailboxes for wider or targeted holes.

### Limitations and Considerations
- **Environmental Impact**: Creates temporary craters and resuspends sediment (turbidity). This has led to regulatory scrutiny, permits, and fines in sensitive areas (e.g., seagrass beds). Modern operations adjust RPMs, use shorter durations, or combine with hand methods to minimize harm.
- **Not for All Sites**: Less effective on hard bottoms, reefs, or very deep water. Requires good anchoring and weather control.
- **Safety**: Strong currents in the hole, potential for collapsing sand, and boat positioning hazards.

**Mel Fisher and the Nuestra Señora de Atocha Salvage: The Epic 16-Year Quest.**

The discovery of the *Nuestra Señora de Atocha* (a 1622 Spanish treasure galleon) by Mel Fisher and Treasure Salvors, Inc. is one of the most famous and valuable shipwreck recoveries in history. ### Historical Context: The 1622 Sinking
- The *Atocha*, part of the Tierra Firme Fleet, sank on September 6, 1622, during a hurricane in the Florida Keys (between Key West and the Dry Tortugas/Marquesas area).
- Cargo: Vast riches from South America — silver bars/coins (pieces of eight), gold, Colombian emeralds, artifacts, copper, indigo, etc. Over 265 people aboard; only 5 survived initially.
- Spanish salvage attempts (using diving bells and enslaved divers) recovered some from the sister ship *Santa Margarita* but failed on the deeper *Atocha*. A later hurricane scattered the wreck.

**Approximate Location**: Around 24°31′53″N 82°49′50″W (or variations like 24°31.5′N 82°20′W in older records), in ~50-55 feet of water. The site is scattered; the "motherlode" (main cargo hold) was the key find.

### Mel Fisher's Search (1969–1985)
Mel Fisher (1922–1998), a former chicken farmer and dive shop owner, moved to Florida and dedicated his life to treasure hunting. With historian Dr. Eugene Lyon (who researched Spanish archives in Seville), the team followed manifests and scatter patterns.

**Timeline Highlights**:
- **Early 1970s**: Found initial traces (silver bars in 1973 matching manifests; bronze cannons in 1975 identified as *Atocha*).
- **July 20, 1985**: Son Kane Fisher (aboard the *Dauntless*) radioed headquarters: **"Put away the charts; we’ve found the main pile!"** or "Today’s the day!" The motherlode appeared as a "reef of silver bars" stacked like cordwood.
- 16+ years of searching involved hundreds of crew, investors, and relentless effort across vast areas.

**Personal Tragedies**: On July 20, 1975 (ironically the same date as the later discovery), son Dirk Fisher, his wife Angel, and diver Rick Gage died when their salvage boat capsized/sank due to bilge pump failure near the site. Fisher and the team persisted despite this devastating loss.

### Salvage Techniques
Fisher’s team pioneered and refined methods still used today (building on 1715 Fleet work):
- **Research & Survey**: Spanish archives, magnetometers (for iron/ferrous objects like cannons/anchors), side-scan sonar for seabed mapping.
- **Prop-Wash Deflectors ("Mailboxes")**: Fisher’s key innovation — large tubes over boat propellers redirect clear surface water downward to excavate sand craters, improve visibility, and expose artifacts. Boats anchored securely; engines in forward gear.
- **Diving & Recovery**: Hand-fanning, metal detectors, careful excavation in ~55 ft water. Systematic grid searches.
- **Documentation**: Artifacts cataloged, conserved (e.g., electrolytic reduction for silver coins).

These techniques turned murky, sandy bottoms into productive dig sites.

- **Scale**: ~40+ tons of silver/gold, 100,000–114,000 silver coins, ~1,000 silver ingots/bars, gold coins/bars/chains, emeralds (including large Muzo specimens), artifacts (porcelain, astrolabes, weapons, personal items). Estimated modern value: $400–450 million+.
