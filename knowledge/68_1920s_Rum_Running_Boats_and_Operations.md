### Bill McCoy’s Notable Boats
William "Bill" McCoy, a legendary rum runner, operated several vessels during Prohibition, known for their quality and his refusal to dilute liquor, earning the phrase "the real McCoy." His boats were primarily schooners adapted for smuggling, and one was famously captured by the U.S. Coast Guard. Here are his key vessels:

1. **Arethusa (Renamed Tomoka)**
   - **Type**: Gloucester knockabout schooner
   - **Years Active**: Early 1920s–1923
   - **Details**: Purchased at auction by McCoy, Arethusa was renamed Tomoka to register under British registry, reducing U.S. jurisdiction risks. McCoy modified her with a larger auxiliary engine, a concealed Colt-Browning machine gun, and refitted fish pens to hold contraband (up to 5,000 cases of liquor). She was a mainstay on Rum Row, operating from Nassau, Bahamas, to the New Jersey coast.
   - **Fate**: Captured by the U.S. Coast Guard Cutter Seneca on November 15, 1923, in international waters off New Jersey. McCoy attempted to flee with a Coast Guard boarding party aboard, firing his machine gun, but surrendered after Seneca fired warning shots and a shell near the hull.[](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_McCoy_%28bootlegger%29)[](https://islandlifenc.com/the-history-of-the-most-famous-rum-runner/)
   - **Significance**: Tomoka was one of the most famous rum-running vessels, central to McCoy’s operations and the establishment of Rum Row, where large ships anchored just beyond the 3-mile (later 12-mile) U.S. territorial limit to sell to contact boats.

2. **Henry L. Marshall**
   - **Type**: Schooner
   - **Years Active**: Early 1920s
   - **Details**: Could carry 1,500 wooden cases or 3,000 burlap "hams" (six-bottle packages wrapped in straw). Used for runs from Nassau to Rum Row, primarily off New Jersey. McCoy’s instructions kept her 25 nautical miles offshore to avoid detection, but the captain’s disregard led to issues, making it a target for the Coast Guard.
   - **Fate**: Impounded in 1921 by the Coast Guard Cutter Seneca off Atlantic City after the captain bragged to an undercover agent, leading to McCoy’s indictment.[](https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2020/february/real-mccoy)[](https://www.boatus.com/expert-advice/expert-advice-archive/2015/june/the-real-mccoy)

### The "Cigarette" Boat Context
The term "Cigarette" boat refers to high-speed powerboats, but during the 1920s, the modern Cigarette brand (founded later by Don Aronow in the 1960s) did not exist. However, the term may be confused with the fast contact boats used in rum-running, which were precursors to later go-fast boats like Cigarettes. These 1920s contact boats were not called "Cigarettes" but shared traits: sleek, powerful, and designed to outrun Coast Guard vessels.

- **Contact Boats (Jersey Sea Skiffs, Speedboats)**:
  - **Description**: Small, open boats (20–40 feet), often mahogany speedboats or lapstrake skiffs, built for speed and agility. Examples include Jersey sea skiffs (28-foot open boats with 60-hp Pierce Arrow car engines) and modified fishing skiffs.[](https://www.sailmagazine.com/cruising/profile-king-rum-row-real-mccoy)[](https://wavetrain.net/2012/08/27/bill-mccoy-the-king-of-rum-row/)
  - **Role**: These boats left shore to meet motherships on Rum Row, purchased liquor, and sped back to inlets, beaches, or trucks for distribution. They operated at night or in fog to evade detection, using local knowledge of waterways.
  - **Engines**: Many were powered by surplus World War I Liberty engines (500-hp V12 aircraft engines), enabling speeds up to 35 mph, far outpacing Coast Guard boats (24 mph). Liberty engines were government surplus, sold cheaply ($100 each), making them ideal for rum runners.[](http://powerandmotoryacht.com/at-sea/the-rumrunners/)[](https://www.liboatingworld.com/single-post/2017/01/12/Rum-Runners-Adventures)
  - **Example**: The **Black Witch**, a 25-foot Hacker Craft speedboat from the early 1920s, used a 150-hp Hemi engine, reaching 32 mph fully loaded. It was never caught, using tactics like posing as a Coast Guard boat to steal other runners’ dumped liquor.[](https://www.liboatingworld.com/single-post/2017/01/12/Rum-Runners-Adventures)

### Shore-to-Offshore Dynamics on Rum Row
Rum Row was a floating marketplace beyond the 3-mile (later 12-mile) U.S. territorial limit, stretching from New York to Atlantic City, with up to 100 ships at its peak. The process worked as follows:

- **Motherships**: Large vessels like McCoy’s Tomoka anchored in international waters, legally selling liquor to contact boats. They carried thousands of cases, packed in wooden crates or "hams" (six-bottle burlap sacks). McCoy used signals (e.g., no British ensign meant open for business) and bright lights to guide buyers.[](https://sherilyndecter.com/the-real-mccoy/)[](https://www.boatus.com/expert-advice/expert-advice-archive/2015/june/the-real-mccoy)
- **Contact Boats**: Small, fast boats (skiffs, speedboats) left shore, often from hidden inlets or beaches in places like Freeport, NY, or Cape May, NJ. They paid in cash (e.g., bundles of $20 bills), loaded liquor, and raced back, using local knowledge to avoid patrols. Some used float planes or flying boats for transfers.[](https://prohibition.themobmuseum.org/the-history/the-rise-of-organized-crime/rum-running/)[](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rum-running)
- **Tactics**: Rum runners dumped liquor overboard in salt-and-cork-weighted bags for later retrieval if pursued. They ran without lights, risking wrecks on sandbars or reefs. Some, like McCoy, used machine guns to deter hijackers or Coast Guard boardings.[](http://powerandmotoryacht.com/at-sea/the-rumrunners/)[](https://islandlifenc.com/the-history-of-the-most-famous-rum-runner/)
- **Challenges**: The Coast Guard, bolstered by $14 million in funding, upgraded its fleet with "six-bitter" patrol boats and cutters like Seneca, but struggled to match rum runners’ speed and cunning. By 1926, enforcement tightened, pushing Rum Row farther out and reducing profitability.[](https://sherilyndecter.com/the-real-mccoy/)[](https://prohibition.themobmuseum.org/the-history/the-rise-of-organized-crime/rum-running/)
