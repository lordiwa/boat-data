## USA History Deep Dive (Yacht-Focused)

**Florida origins — the "wrecking" era (1540s–1921).** Florida Keys wrecking was America's first organized marine salvage industry. Spanish galleons (1540s onward) littered the reefs with treasure. In the early 1800s Bahamian wreckers (descendants of Tories) and New England fishermen turned salvors. The first to reach a wreck became "wreck master" and directed cargo and ship recovery plus crew rescue. Goods were auctioned in Key West (after 1820s US law requiring federal court valuation). Key West became the wealthiest US city per capita (1830s to early 1900s) on the salvage economy of shipyards, chandleries, and auctions, averaging one wreck per week. The last federal wrecking license was issued in 1921; lighthouses and steamships ended the era. The direct link to modern yacht salvage is the same reward principles (the 1989 Convention's ten factors) and pure-salvage roots.

**Broader USA yacht and marine salvage history.**

- Pre-1989: common-law "no cure, no pay" plus the Blackwall factors (1869 Supreme Court case).
- WWII era: massive US Navy salvages (Pearl Harbor; USS Oklahoma refloated). Private firms such as Merritt-Chapman & Scott dominated the Atlantic and Gulf coasts.
- Modern (post-1989 Convention ratification): Unified Command system (owner, USCG, salvor) and a pollution focus (Article 14 special compensation).
- Yacht-era shifts: the post-WWII recreational boom produced dedicated yacht salvors (TowBoatU.S., Sea Tow). Treasure salvors (Mel Fisher, Atocha, 1985) influenced commercial rules. In 2026, climate-driven storms and superyacht growth push Florida operators into high-value yacht work (Resolve, Dann Ocean).
