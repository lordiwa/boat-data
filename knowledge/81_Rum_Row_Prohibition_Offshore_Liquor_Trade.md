In 1920-1922, before enforcement ramped up, it was low-risk: load up at Rum Row, dodge a sleepy patrol, and unload at a dock or beach. A 1924 *Motor Boating* article by Henry Clay Foster noted how rum runners’ need for speed spurred boat innovation, turning hobbyists into smugglers overnight.

- **Immigrant Communities**: Long Island’s boatmen often came from Italian, Greek, or Portuguese stock—maritime folks who’d settled the South Shore. In Newport, Rhode Island, Charlie Travers, a Portuguese-descended rum runner, captained the *Black Duck*, a speedboat that became infamous in 1929. These guys brought Old World seamanship to the game.

- **Profiles in Courage (and Greed)**: Take John Gross, an African-American captain of the *Ida C. Robinson*. In 1923, he ran liquor from Chesapeake Bay to New York via canals, commanding a mixed-race crew—a rarity then. Or consider Rudolph Wylk of Rockville Centre, Long Island, a big player who owned multiple boats like the *Mary Mother Superior*. He wasn’t just a runner; he was a businessman, commissioning vessels and pleading guilty to smuggling in 1925, serving four years.

Data’s spotty—Prohibition records focus more on seizures than biographies—but Ellen NicKenzie Lawson’s research on 250 seized NYC-area boats (1920-1933) gives us glimpses. Of those, dozens were small contact boats crewed by these shore runners, often unnamed in reports, just listed by vessel: *Dorothy M. Smart* (Long Island-owned, four sailors rescued from hijackers), *Paul Jones* (seized off Jones Inlet), or *Maud S. II* (Bay Shore shrimp boat hiding liquor). These crews were small—two to six men—risking jail or bullets for a payday.

### The Boat Builders: Early 1920s Innovators

Now, the boat builders—those early 1920s craftsmen on Long Island and beyond—were the unsung heroes of Rum Row’s shore-to-offshore pipeline. They took fishing boats and pleasure craft and turned them into smuggling legends, often using those World War I Liberty engines you saw on the History Channel. Here’s the lineup:

- **Scopinich Family (Freeport, Long Island)**: Fred Scopinich Sr. founded Freeport Point Shipyard around 1920 at Woodcleft Canal. Early on, he built for baymen-turned-runners, crafting shallow-draft boats with beefed-up hulls. By 1923, he was dual-dealing—supplying rum runners with speedboats (some packing twin Liberty V-12s, 400 hp each, hitting 35 knots) *and* the Coast Guard with patrol boats. A restored 1930 Scopinich rum runner still cruises today, proof of their durability. Fred Jr. moved the yard to East Quogue in 1956, but the Prohibition legacy stuck.

- **Maresca Family (Freeport)**: Philip Maresca, originally a printer, switched to boatbuilding in the 1920s after moving from Hampton Bays to Freeport. His yard churned out scallop boats and trawlers, some seized on Rum Row—like one that sank under tow in 1924. Maresca’s boats were workhorses, not speed demons, but runners loved their reliability for mid-range hauls.

- **Patchogue Builders (Gil Smith & Others)**: Gil Smith, a late-19th-century catboat master, was winding down by the 1920s, but his flat-bottom designs influenced local yards. Patchogue’s Weeks family, led by Frank M. Weeks, built rugged boats like the *Onion* (1898), and by the 1920s, their yard on Riverview Court was tweaking fishing craft for rum runs—oak and pine hulls, sometimes retrofitted with surplus engines.

- **Engine Retrofits**: The Liberty engines—27 liters, 1,650 rpm—flooded the market post-1918. Long Island yards like Consolidated Yachts (Morrisville) and Jakobson (Oyster Bay) bolted these beasts onto sleek hulls. A 1924 *Maud F.* bust off Fire Island revealed 300-hp Liberty motors and $30,000 in upgrades (about $500,000 today), hinting at Scopinich-level craftsmanship. These boats could outrun the Coast Guard’s early 12-knot “six-bitters.”

No exact production stats survive—shipyards didn’t log “rum runner” orders—but we know demand exploded. A 1922 Federal Prohibition Commission report counted 60+ mother ships off New Jersey alone, each needing multiple contact boats. Long Island likely built hundreds of these craft from 1920-1924, before bigger Nova Scotia freighters took over.

### The Rum Runners Themselves: Data and Stories

Hard numbers on the runners are elusive—most avoided the spotlight, and Coast Guard logs prioritize ships over crews. But here’s what we can stitch together:

- **Volume and Value**: From 1920-1923, Rum Row’s “golden years,” a single contact boat might haul 50-250 cases per run (600-3,000 bottles). A case fetched $50-$100 onshore, netting a runner $2,500-$25,000 per trip (up to $400,000 today). Lawson’s 250 seized boats carried everything from 50 cases (*Maud F.*) to 750-case averages for bigger hauls.

- **Risk and Reward**: A captain could earn $200,000 a year—30 times the Coast Guard Commandant’s $6,000 salary. Crewmen made $50-$100 per run, dwarfing the $30/week of a legit seaman. But risks were high: the *Black Duck* lost three men to machine-gun fire in 1929; the *Linwood* crew burned their boat in 1923 to dodge arrest.

- **Key Figures**:
  - *Bill McCoy*: The kingpin who started it all. His *Tomoka* ran 1921-1923, hauling thousands of cases from Nassau to New York. Caught in ’23, he did nine months and retired rich.
  - *Charlie Travers*: Newport’s *Black Duck* captain, wounded in ’29, survived to tell the tale. His boat hit 30+ knots, Liberty-powered.
  - *R.W. Wylk*: Long Island’s mini-mogul, owned boats like *Dorothy M. Smart* and *Mary Mother Superior*. Jailed in ’25 but left a smuggling blueprint.

- **Casualties**: The Coast Guard nabbed over 500 vessels by 1933, but runner deaths are hazy—dozens likely died in shootouts or wrecks. Twelve Coast Guardsmen died enforcing Prohibition, per official counts.

### Why It Worked Early On

In 1920-1922, the Coast Guard was a skeleton crew—old ships, no budget, stretched thin by rescues and border duties. Rum runners ruled the night, using local knowledge and souped-up boats. By 1924, when the Coast Guard got 90 six-bitters (24 knots) and Navy destroyers (30+ knots), the game tightened. But those early years? Pure chaos—and profit—for the shore-to-offshore crowd.
