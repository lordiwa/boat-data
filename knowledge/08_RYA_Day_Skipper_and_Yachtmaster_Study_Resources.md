Fixed-bottom projects (shallower waters) rely heavily on **jack-up WTIVs** (Wind Turbine Installation Vessels) that self-elevate on legs for stable crane work. Floating wind operations involve:
- **Towing** pre-assembled or partially assembled floating platforms (with or without turbines) to site.

- **Motion-compensated access** (gangways or daughter craft) because both the vessel and floating turbine move independently in waves.

**Detailed Ballasting Process for Submersible Rigs (Mr. Charlie & Similar Designs)**:
1. **Transit**: The barge hull floats on the surface and is towed by tugs to the drilling location (like a ship).
2. **Positioning**: On location, the rig is positioned and anchored or held in place. Precise alignment over the well site is critical.
3. **Ballasting (Flooding)**: Seawater is pumped into ballast tanks in the lower barge hull. This increases weight and lowers the rig gradually. 4. **Landing on Seabed**: The barge sinks until it rests firmly on the seafloor. The 4-foot skirts help penetrate soft sediment for better stability and to prevent scouring. Legs/columns keep the elevated platform (60+ ft above) well clear of waves.
5. **Preloading/Stabilization**: Additional weight (more ballast or equipment) may be added to “preload” the legs into the seabed for firm seating. The rig becomes a stable, bottom-supported platform for drilling.
6. **Drilling**: Operations proceed with the platform elevated above wave action.
7. **De-ballasting (Refloating)**: After the well is complete, water is pumped out of the ballast tanks. The barge refloats, is detached, and towed to the next site.

**Semi-submersible (semi) evolution** represents one of the most important advances in offshore drilling. Unlike earlier **submersible rigs** (e.g., *Mr. Charlie*, 1954), which floated only for transit and then ballasted fully to sit on the seabed, semi-submersibles **remain floating** (partially submerged) while drilling. This provides excellent stability in deeper water and rougher seas because the large submerged pontoons sit below the wave action, minimizing heave, roll, and pitch.

### The Accidental Birth: Blue Water Rig No. 1 (1961)
The semi-submersible concept emerged **by accident** in **1961** in the Gulf of Mexico.

- **Rig**: *Blue Water Rig No. 1* (owned by Blue Water Drilling Company, contracted to **Shell Oil**).
- **Original design**: It was a conventional **submersible** rig (pontoons + columns) meant to be towed and then ballasted to rest on the seabed in shallow water.
- **The key observation**: The pontoons lacked sufficient buoyancy for the full load of rig + consumables. While being towed between locations at a **partial draught** (midway between the top of the pontoons and the underside of the deck), engineers noticed the rig had **remarkably small motions** in waves.
- **Key people**: **Bruce Collipp** of Shell is often credited as the primary inventor/recognizer of the potential. Shell and Blue Water Drilling jointly decided to operate the rig in this **floating/semi-submerged mode** instead of fully ballasting it down.
- **Outcome**: It became the **first semi-submersible drilling unit**. It operated successfully while floating, moored with anchors, and extended workable water depths significantly beyond traditional submersibles.

This discovery proved that a column-stabilized floating platform could achieve good motion characteristics by keeping the main buoyancy (pontoons) well below the surface while the working deck stayed high above the waves.

- **Significance**: This was the **first purpose-built semi-submersible drilling rig**. It proved the concept commercially viable and inspired rapid industry adoption. It remained active until 1992.

### Evolution and Generations of Semi-Submersibles
Semis evolved through several generations as water depths increased, environmental conditions became harsher (especially North Sea), and technology advanced (better mooring, dynamic positioning, larger deck loads, deeper drilling capability).

**Key design principles**:
- **Pontoons** (lower hulls) provide most buoyancy and are submerged.
- **Columns** (stability columns) connect pontoons to the upper deck and provide additional buoyancy/stability.
- **Upper deck/hull** stays well above wave height (“air gap”).
- **Ballasting system**: Seawater is pumped in/out of pontoon and column tanks to adjust draught, trim, and stability. Partial submersion keeps the center of gravity low and reduces wave forces.
- **Station-keeping**: Early units used 8–12 anchor lines (wire/chain/polyester). Later units adopted **Dynamic Positioning (DP2/DP3)** for deep water where anchoring is impractical.

**Generational progression** (rough industry classification):
- **1st Generation (1960s–early 1970s)**: Small, mostly 4–6 columns, limited to ~600–1,000 ft water depth. Examples: *Ocean Driller*, early SEDCO designs. Mostly moored.
- **2nd Generation (1970s)**: Larger, improved stability, North Sea adaptations for harsh weather. More robust mooring.
- **3rd/4th Generation (1980s–1990s)**: Deeper capability (2,000–4,000+ ft), better deck loads, early DP systems. Examples: *Polar Pioneer* (first 4th-gen capable of year-round harsh-environment ops).
- **5th/6th Generation (2000s–present)**: Ultra-deepwater (up to 12,000+ ft water depth, 40,000+ ft drilling depth). Advanced DP3, dual-activity derricks, larger variable deck loads, better motion characteristics, and higher safety/redundancy standards. Many can operate in harsh environments like the North Sea or Gulf of Mexico ultra-deep.

**Major configuration changes**:
- Early: Multiple slender columns + interconnected pontoons.
- Later: Fewer but larger columns (often 4), twin-pontoon or ring-pontoon designs, space-frame trussing for strength.
- Modern trend: Robust 4-legged semis for most ops; 6-legged for extreme conditions.

**Key milestones after 1963**:
- By **1972**: ~30 semi-submersible units worldwide.
- **1970s–1980s**: Rapid growth driven by North Sea development and deeper Gulf of Mexico exploration.
- **1979**: *Sedco 709* — one of the first dynamically positioned (DP) semis.
- **2000**: *Deepwater Nautilus* and similar units set records in ultra-deep water.
- Today: Modern semis can drill in 12,000+ ft water depth with minimal downtime in severe weather.

### Ballasting Process for Semi-Submersibles (vs. Early Submersibles)
Unlike early submersibles (*Mr. Charlie*), which fully submerged the barge to sit on the seabed:

1. **Transit mode**: Pontoons mostly empty or lightly ballasted → rig floats high with good towing speed and stability.
2. **On location**: Positioned using tugs or DP thrusters.
3. **Ballasting**: Seawater is pumped into the lower **pontoons** (and sometimes lower column sections) in a controlled, symmetric manner. This lowers the rig so the pontoons are fully submerged while the columns pierce the water surface and the main deck remains high above waves.
4. **Operating mode**: The rig floats at a stable “semi-submerged” draught. The low center of gravity and submerged pontoons minimize wave-induced motions. Mooring lines or DP thrusters hold position.
5. **De-ballasting**: Pump out water to raise the rig for transit to the next location.

This process is monitored continuously with sensors for list, trim, and stability. Modern rigs have sophisticated automated ballast control systems.

**Britannia** was the **83rd** and final official royal yacht. Since 1997, the British monarchy has had **no official royal yacht**. Occasional proposals for a replacement (including under Boris Johnson) have not materialized, partly due to cost and changing public sentiment.

### Current Situation (2026)
- The British Royal Family does **not** own or operate a dedicated royal yacht.
- They use chartered or loaned vessels for occasional needs, or commercial flights and smaller boats for private travel.
- Smaller royal sailing yachts have been owned privately by family members (e.g., the Queen and Prince Philip owned the racing yacht *Bloodhound* from 1962–1969).
- In 2012, a royal barge called **Gloriana** (88ft, 18-oared) was built for the Diamond Jubilee — the first royal barge in over 250 years — but it is not a full ocean-going yacht.

### Royal Yachts Overview
The British monarchy has commissioned or used **83 royal yachts** since the tradition formally began in **1660** with King Charles II. Early yachts were small, fast sailing vessels for pleasure, racing, and coastal diplomacy. Later ones evolved into large steam- and turbine-powered ships serving as floating palaces for state visits, diplomatic tours, and family holidays.

The most iconic and best-documented is **HMY Britannia** (1954–1997), the last official royal yacht. No replacement has been built as of 2026.

### Expanded Specifications: HMY Britannia (The Most Famous Modern Royal Yacht)
**General Data**:
- **Full Name**: Her Majesty’s Yacht *Britannia*
- **Builder**: John Brown & Company, Clydebank, Scotland (Yard No. 691) — same yard that built *Queen Mary* and *Queen Elizabeth*
- **Ordered**: 4 February 1952 (by King George VI)
- **Launched**: 16 April 1953 by Queen Elizabeth II (she used a bottle of Empire wine instead of champagne)
- **Commissioned**: 11 January 1954
- **Decommissioned**: 11 December 1997
- **Current Status**: Museum ship at Ocean Terminal, Leith, Edinburgh, Scotland

**Technical Specifications**:
- **Length Overall (LOA)**: 412 ft 3 in (125.65 m / 126 m)
- **Beam**: 55 ft (16.8 m)
- **Draught**: 15 ft (4.6 m) to 17 ft (5.2 m) at deep load
- **Gross Tonnage**: 5,769 GT
- **Displacement**: Approximately 4,961–5,862 tons (varies by load)
- **Height**: 123 ft (37 m) to top of mainmast
- **Propulsion**: 2 Pametrada geared steam turbines (12,000 shp / 8,900–8,948 kW total)
- **Speed**:
  - Maximum (trials): 22.5–22.8 knots
  - Service/Cruising: 21 knots
- **Range**: 2,400 nautical miles at 14–21 knots (with 510 tons of fuel)
- **Hull Material**: Steel hull with teak decks
- **Masts**: Three masts (foremast 133 ft / 41 m, mainmast 139 ft / 42 m, mizzenmast 118 ft / 36 m) — used for signals and aesthetics, not sails

**Capacity & Crew**:
- **Guests**: Up to 250 for official receptions/events; more intimate family quarters for the Royal Family
- **Crew**: 21 officers + approximately 250–271 Royal Yachtsmen (many from the Royal Navy). The crew was known for exceptional standards and loyalty.
- **Cabins**: Multiple royal suites, guest cabins, and extensive crew accommodation

**Key Features & Design**:
