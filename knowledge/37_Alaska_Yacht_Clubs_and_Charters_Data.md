Alaska offers world-class **fishing charters**, with prime targets being **salmon** (king/Chinook, silver/Coho, sockeye, pink, chum) and **halibut** (often called "barn doors" for their size), plus rockfish, lingcod, and more. The season typically runs May–September, peaking in summer for runs and calmer weather. Charters range from half-day (4–6 hours) to full-day or multi-day, often including gear, bait, filleting/freezing/shipping of catch, and wildlife sightings (whales, eagles, bears).

Key hotspots:
- **Homer** — "Halibut Capital of the World" (Cook Inlet; excellent for big halibut combos with salmon/rockfish).
- **Seward** — Kenai Fjords area (salmon, halibut, lingcod; scenic with national park views).
- **Ketchikan** — "Salmon Capital of the World" (Inside Passage; great for cruise passengers, salmon/halibut combos).
- **Juneau** — Inside Passage (salmon, halibut; often combined with whale watching).
- **Kenai Peninsula rivers** (e.g., Kenai, Kasilof) — River salmon fishing (float trips, guided).

Many charters are private/small group (4–6 people max per boat), family-friendly, and no experience needed. Prices vary by season, duration, group size, and type (e.g., $250–$500+ per person for day trips; higher for private/luxury). Book early for peak July–August.

Organized primarily by the Royal Victoria Yacht Club (RVYC) since 1951 (with shared hosting earlier), the race draws its name from Swiftsure Bank and the historic Swiftsure Lightship, which marked the entrance to the strait.

### Origins and Early History
Sailing races in the Victoria area date back to the late 1850s, when Royal Navy boats competed against early colonial vessels. Organized yachting grew with the founding of clubs like RVYC (1892) and Royal Vancouver Yacht Club (RVYC, 1903). The modern Swiftsure concept emerged in the late 1920s amid growing interest in long-distance offshore racing between Vancouver Island and the U.S. Pacific Northwest.

The inaugural **Swiftsure Lightship Classic Race** was held on May 31–June 1, 1930. Just six boats started from Cadboro Bay near Victoria, racing out the Strait of Juan de Fuca to Swiftsure Bank (about 15–20 miles offshore), rounding a mark near the lightship, and returning—a roughly 138-nautical-mile course. The sole prize was a trophy donated by the Royal Vancouver Yacht Club. The event was a collaboration between RVYC, Royal Vancouver Yacht Club, and Seattle Yacht Club, reflecting cross-border rivalries and camaraderie.

The race was held sporadically in the early years: again in 1931 and 1934, but paused during the Great Depression and World War II. Post-war revival came in 1950, with consistent annual running from then on.

- **1950s–1960s**: The race formalized under RVYC's primary stewardship from 1951. A shorter **Juan de Fuca Race** (about 80–100 nm) was introduced as an alternative or companion event. The **1979 Fastnet Race disaster** remains the deadliest event in modern offshore yacht racing history. During the 27th edition of this prestigious biennial race organized by the Royal Ocean Racing Club (RORC), a sudden, unforecasted storm on August 13–14, 1979, turned the 605-nautical-mile course into a survival ordeal. Of the **303 yachts** that started from Cowes, Isle of Wight, on August 11, only **85 finished**. It triggered the largest peacetime rescue operation in British waters and prompted sweeping global reforms in yacht design, safety equipment, and race management.

The storm resulted from an unpredicted rapid intensification of a low-pressure system (designated "Low Y") in the Celtic Sea. Key factors:

- **Rapid Deepening ("Bomb Cyclone")**: The low deepened explosively from ~995 mb to 979 mb in 12–18 hours on August 13–14, driven by a jet stream venturi effect that created a "partial vacuum" and accelerated winds.
- **Wind Speeds**: Sustained Force 10–11 (48–63+ knots; gusts estimated 70+ knots). Fastnet Lighthouse recorded Force 9–10 (41–55 knots) from 2300 August 13 to 0400 August 14, dropping to Force 8 by 1100.
- **Wave Heights**: Significant wave height ~33 feet (10 m); rogue breakers 50–60 feet (15–18 m) in conflicting directions (southwesterly swell met northwesterly wind waves). Shallow Labadie Bank amplified chaos.
- **Forecast Failure**: UK Met Office predicted Force 6–7 increasing to Force 8; no advance warning of the bomb cyclone due to limited satellite data and models. Modern re-forecasts (ECMWF, DWD, WRF) show the storm was predictable 72 hours ahead today.
- **Timing**: Hit the mid-fleet hardest in the Celtic Sea near Fastnet Rock, where confused seas from wind shifts and shallows made conditions "especially destructive."

The storm lasted ~20 hours, peaking overnight August 13–14, with barometric drops creating freak waves that overwhelmed even experienced crews.

### Quick Summary Insights

- **Pure racing vs. rally**:
  - RORC Transatlantic, Route du Rhum, Transat Jacques Vabre, Mini Transat = competitive races with records, prizes, professional skippers.
  - ARC / ARC+ = non-competitive rallies for cruising yachts (fun, safety-focused, large fleets).

- **Solo vs. Crewed**:
  - Solo: Route du Rhum, Mini Transat, Vendée Globe (full circumnavigation).
  - Doublehanded: Transat Jacques Vabre, some Mini Transat legs.
  - Crewed: RORC Transatlantic, ARC, most classic transatlantic races.

- **Technology level**:
  - High-tech: Route du Rhum (Ultim, IMOCA foiling), Transat Jacques Vabre.
  - Retro/low-tech: Golden Globe Race (pre-1988 designs, no electronics).
  - Modern IRC: RORC Transatlantic (monohulls + multihulls).

- **Difficulty / Prestige ranking** (subjective, offshore sailor consensus):
  1. Vendée Globe (full solo circumnavigation, Southern Ocean).
  2. Route du Rhum (solo transatlantic, French icon).
  3. Transat Jacques Vabre (doublehanded, strategic).
  4. RORC Transatlantic (crewed tradewinds classic).
  5. Mini Transat (smallest boats, extreme).
  6. ARC / ARC+ (cruiser rally, least competitive).

- **Geographic focus**:
  - Northern Hemisphere East-to-West: RORC Transatlantic, ARC.
  - French Atlantic: Route du Rhum, Transat Jacques Vabre, Mini Transat.
  - Southern Hemisphere: Cape2Rio (Cape Town → Rio).

For datayacht.com, the RORC Transatlantic Race is the most accessible and "classic" crewed tradewinds transatlantic race, while Route du Rhum and Transat Jacques Vabre represent the high-performance solo/doublehanded elite level. The ARC is the best entry point for non-racers.
