Law 103 absolved the borrower if robbed by enemies during the journey, provided an oath (affidavit) confirmed the theft. These provisions shifted risk from the borrower (merchant/shipowner) to the lender (investor), functioning as de facto insurance: the high interest premium covered potential total loss, while successful voyages rewarded the risk-taker. Bottomry thus combined lending with risk-bearing, making it a hybrid of modern loan and insurance.

Merchants sought alternatives: risk-sharing without interest disguised as loans.

General average remained universal, inherited from Rhodian law via Roman Digest and Byzantine Rhodian Sea-Law (7th–8th centuries). But for total loss prevention, premium insurance emerged: underwriters (often wealthy merchants or syndicates) received a fixed premium upfront to cover potential loss, regardless of voyage outcome. This separated insurance from lending, enabling specialization.

### Earliest Evidence: Italian City-States and the First Policies (14th Century)

Italy pioneered true marine insurance. Genoa, Venice, Florence, and Pisa led, driven by trade with Byzantium, the Levant, and Catalonia.

The earliest surviving marine insurance contract dates to **Genoa in 1343** (not 1347 as often cited). Notary Tommaso Casanova drafted it on March 18, 1343. It insured goods on a voyage, with underwriters accepting risk for a premium. By 1347, Genoa had multiple such contracts; premiums varied intuitively by route, season, and peril (e.g., higher for wartime or winter voyages).

Genoa's market exploded post-Black Death (1348), as surviving merchants consolidated wealth. Policies were notarial acts: merchant (insured) paid premium to underwriters (often multiple for risk-sharing). Underwriters pledged repayment on loss. Premiums (e.g., 5–15% of value) reflected perceived risk. Genoa's 1369 decree by Doge Gabriele Adorno regulated insurance, imposing fines for non-compliance with usury bans and standardizing terms.

Venice followed closely. Venetian merchants insured wool from England (e.g., 1455 policy by Pier Francesco de’ Medici). Venice's Rialto became a hub; policies were standardized by the 15th century. Florence contributed via merchant-bankers like Medici, who insured shipments.

Pisa and other Tuscan ports adopted similar practices. The first known Dutch/English policies insured Italian trade, with Genoese underwriters in Bruges (1370 transaction).

### Barcelona and the Ordinances: Early Regulation (1435 and Beyond)

Barcelona, a major Iberian hub, formalized insurance early. The **Book of the Consulate of the Sea** (Llibre del Consolat de Mar), compiled c. 1258–1494, included maritime customs but focused on general average, jettison, and salvage. True insurance ordinances came later.

The **Ordinances of the Councilmen of Barcelona** (1435) were pivotal—the oldest surviving law regulating marine insurance premiums explicitly. Proclaimed November 21, 1435, they required premiums be stated clearly in contracts, limited insurable risks, and prohibited excessive speculation. A 1484 update expanded rules, influencing Iberian and Mediterranean practice. Barcelona's ordinances addressed usury concerns, ensuring insurance was a premium-based transfer, not disguised loan.

These laws standardized wording, dispute resolution (via consuls/merchants' courts), and prohibited insuring lives of princes/pope (echoing earlier bans). Barcelona's influence spread via Catalan trade networks.

### Bottomry and Respondentia in Medieval Europe: Persistence and Transition

Bottomry/respondentia endured alongside insurance. In medieval Europe, they were formalized under Church oversight. Bottomry secured by ship hull; respondentia by cargo. High interest (20–40%) compensated risk, but usury debates persisted. Italian states regulated them; e.g., Genoa's 1467 statutes.

In Northern Europe, Bruges (1370 Genoese underwriter record) and Antwerp adopted Italian models. By 15th century, Antwerp's Bourse facilitated insurance; policies for Italian trade appeared. Bottomry declined as premium insurance offered cleaner risk transfer without debt.

### Institutional and Legal Evolution: Guilds, Notaries, and Courts

Notaries drafted policies as legal instruments. Underwriters formed informal syndicates (risk-sharing pools). Disputes resolved by merchants' consuls or special courts (e.g., Venice's Consoli dei Mercanti, 1436; Genoa's Rota, 1520).

Guilds and family networks underwrote risks. Premium variation reflected actuarial intuition: route, season, war, vessel type.

### Spread to Northern Europe and Legacy

By late 15th century, insurance reached Bruges, Antwerp, London, and Amsterdam. Early English/Dutch policies insured Italian trade. This laid foundations for Lloyd's (17th century) and modern markets.

Medieval developments birthed premium-based marine insurance, separating it from lending, standardizing terms, and regulating via ordinances. From Genoa's 1343 policy to Barcelona's 1435 rules, this era transformed risk management into a commercial instrument, enabling global trade expansion.
