### Executive Summary
Johnson and Evinrude were iconic American outboard motor brands that dominated recreational and commercial boating for much of the 20th century. Founded separately—Evinrude in 1907 by Ole Evinrude in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, and Johnson Motor Company in the early 1920s by the Johnson brothers—they merged under Outboard Marine Corporation (OMC) in the 1930s. OMC became a Fortune 500 powerhouse, producing millions of motors, expanding into boats, lawn mowers, and more.

The brands represented innovation in two-stroke engine technology, from lightweight aluminum designs to advanced direct-injection systems. However, intense competition, regulatory pressures on emissions, costly technological bets (notably FICHT), and internal challenges led to OMC's bankruptcy in 2000. Bombardier Recreational Products (BRP) acquired the brands, re-engineered them into E-TEC, but discontinued Evinrude production in 2020. Johnson was phased out earlier.

### The Landmark Patent: U.S. Patent No. 1,001,260 – "Marine Propulsion Mechanism"

On September 16, 1910, Ole Evinrude filed his application for what would become one of the foundational patents in marine engineering history. The U.S. Patent Office granted it on August 22, 1911, as Patent No. 1,001,260. This document formalized the design principles that made the detachable outboard motor practical and commercially viable.

**Patent Abstract and Core Object:**
Ole Evinrude described his invention as providing "simple, economical and efficient and preferably portable propulsion mechanism for ready detachable connection with the stern or a side of a rowboat or other small craft and to provide for ready adjustment of the propeller portion of the mechanism to a suitable angle and depth with respect to said craft."

**Key Technical Elements (from the Specification):**
- **Engine Configuration**: A single-cylinder, two-cycle (two-stroke) internal-combustion engine with a horizontal cylinder (No. 5) and vertical crankshaft arrangement. The piston (No. 6) connected to the crankshaft (No. 7), which extended downward.
- **Mounting System**: A swing-frame (No. 13) with bowed upper ends and clamp-screws (No. 14) for secure, adjustable attachment to boat transoms of varying angles (flaring or vertical sterns).
- **Driveshaft Design**: A telescoping tube system (fixed tube No. 15 depending from the engine, sliding tube No. 16 connected to the gear-case No. 17). This allowed vertical adjustment for propeller depth.
- **Steering and Control**: A tiller (No. 19) with a split tubular shank that clamped onto the sliding tube, enabling easy directional control.
- **Power Transmission**: Bevel gears in the lower unit transferred power from the vertical driveshaft to the horizontal propeller shaft.
- **Cooling and Fuel**: Water pump (piston No. 29 driven by cam on propeller shaft gear) for cooling; integrated gasoline tank (No. 10). Flywheel with crank handle (No. 9) for starting.
- **Adjustability Innovations**: A collar (No. 18) with V-shaped slotted extension for angular and depth positioning without binding. Set-screws secured adjustments.

The patent drawings (two sheets) illustrated side, rear, and sectional views, highlighting the portability and adaptability that distinguished it from earlier experimental designs like Waterman’s four-stroke outboard. Claims focused on the combination of the engine, extensible shaft, swing-frame, adjustable collar, telescoping tubes, tiller, propeller, and gearing.

**Historical Significance**: This patent protected the core architecture—vertical crankshaft, detachable clamp, adjustable lower unit—that became the industry standard. ### Founding and the Johnson Brothers: Early Ingenuity (Pre-1922)
The Johnson brothers—Louis (Lou), Harry, Julius, and Clarence—were born in the late 1800s to Soren and Bertha Johnson. In 1908, working in a barn in Terre Haute, Indiana, they built their first single-cylinder, two-cycle (two-stroke) marine engine: a 3 HP monster weighing 150 pounds.
