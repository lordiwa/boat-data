This is the **U-505**, a Type IXC U-boat from World War II, and it’s housed at the **Museum of Science and Industry in Chicago**. Here’s the story of how it got there:

- **Capture of U-505**: On June 4, 1944, the U-505 was captured by the U.S. Navy Task Group 22.3 off the coast of West Africa, near Cape Verde. The USS Chatelain, a destroyer, detected the submarine via sonar, and after a coordinated attack involving depth charges and aircraft, the U-505 was forced to surface. The German crew abandoned ship, and a boarding party from the USS Pillsbury successfully captured it. This was a significant event, as it was one of the few U-boats taken intact during the war, providing valuable intelligence on German submarine technology and codes. The capture was kept secret to prevent the Germans from knowing their codes were compromised.[](https://www.msichicago.org/explore/whats-here/exhibits/u-505-submarine)[](https://militaryhistorynow.com/2014/05/31/permission-to-come-aboard-four-surviving-u-boats-you-can-actually-visit/)

- **Acquisition by the USA**: After its capture, the U-505 was towed to Bermuda for study by the U.S. Navy. Post-war, in 1954, it was donated to the Museum of Science and Industry in Chicago, where it remains a permanent exhibit. Visitors can explore the submarine and its accompanying exhibit, which details its wartime history and capture. The U-505 is the only German U-boat on display in the United States.[](https://www.msichicago.org/explore/whats-here/exhibits/u-505-submarine)[](https://militaryhistorynow.com/2014/05/31/permission-to-come-aboard-four-surviving-u-boats-you-can-actually-visit/)

Some were sunk in U.S. waters, and their wrecks remain off the East Coast and Gulf of Mexico (e.g., U-576 off North Carolina). The story of the **U-505**’s capture and its tow to Bermuda is one of the most dramatic episodes of World War II naval history, showcasing U.S. Navy ingenuity and bravery. Below is a detailed account of the U-505’s capture on June 4, 1944, and its subsequent tow to Bermuda, focusing on the key events, people, and challenges involved.

### Background on U-505
- **Construction and Service**: The U-505, a Type IXC U-boat, was built at the Deutsche Werft shipyard in Hamburg, Germany, launched on May 25, 1941, and commissioned on August 26, 1941. Measuring 76.76 meters (251 feet 10 inches) in length, it was designed for long-range operations, capable of crossing the Atlantic to target Allied shipping. By 1944, U-505 had completed 12 patrols under three commanders, sinking eight Allied ships but also enduring repeated mechanical issues and damage from Allied attacks. Its final commander, **Oberleutnant zur See Harald Lange**, took over in late 1943.

- **Context in 1944**: By mid-1944, the Battle of the Atlantic had turned against Germany. Allied anti-submarine tactics, including improved sonar, air patrols, and hunter-killer groups, made U-boat operations increasingly perilous. The U-505 was operating off the West African coast, tasked with disrupting Allied supply lines, but it was plagued by mechanical problems and low crew morale.

### The Capture: June 4, 1944
The capture of the U-505 was a meticulously executed operation by **U.S. Navy Task Group 22.3**, led by **Captain Daniel V. Gallery** aboard the escort carrier **USS Guadalcanal**. The task group, operating in the Atlantic near Cape Verde, consisted of the Guadalcanal and five destroyer escorts: **USS Pillsbury**, **USS Pope**, **USS Flaherty**, **USS Chatelain**, and **USS Jenks**. Here’s how the capture unfolded:

1. **Detection**:
   - On June 4, 1944, at around 11:00 AM, the USS Chatelain’s sonar detected a submerged contact approximately 150 miles west of Cape Blanco, French West Africa. The task group was patrolling an area where intelligence (partly from codebreaking efforts like Ultra) suggested U-boat activity.
   - The U-505 was running submerged at about 150 feet, unaware it had been detected. The Chatelain confirmed the contact as a submarine and signaled the task group to converge.

2. **Initial Attack**:
   - The Chatelain launched a salvo of **hedgehog depth charges**, which missed, followed by a pattern of **depth charges**. These exploded close to U-505, causing significant damage: the submarine’s rudder was jammed, its electrical systems failed, and water began flooding the engine room. Believing the boat was doomed, Commander Lange ordered the crew to surface and abandon ship.
   - At around 11:15 AM, U-505 surfaced, and the German crew began evacuating, believing they had set scuttling charges to sink the submarine. However, the charges were not properly activated, leaving the U-boat intact.

3. **Boarding Operation**:
   - As U-505 surfaced, the task group opened fire with machine guns and small-caliber weapons to keep the German crew away from the deck guns and to hasten their abandonment. The Germans fled into life rafts, leaving the submarine vulnerable.
   - Captain Gallery, recognizing the rare opportunity to capture a U-boat, ordered a boarding party. A nine-man team from the USS Pillsbury, led by **Lieutenant Albert David**, quickly boarded the U-505 via a whaleboat. This was a high-risk operation, as the submarine could sink or explode from scuttling charges.
   - The boarding party found the U-505 abandoned but flooding rapidly through an open sea strainer (a valve left open by the crew). **Seaman Arthur Schwartz** and others worked frantically to close valves and stop leaks. They also discovered and disarmed scuttling charges, preventing the submarine’s destruction.
   - The team secured critical materials, including codebooks, an **Enigma machine**, and other cryptographic documents, which were vital for Allied intelligence. They also rigged the U-505 for towing by attaching a cable from the Pillsbury.

4. **Challenges During Capture**:
   - The U-505 was in poor condition, with water flooding the engine room and the submarine listing heavily. The boarding party’s quick actions to close hatches and valves kept it afloat.
   - The operation was unprecedented: capturing an enemy warship at sea was rare, and the risk of the U-505 sinking or exploding made it a race against time.
   - The German crew, now in life rafts, was taken prisoner by the task group. One German sailor, Hans Goebeler, later died of wounds, but 58 others were captured.

### Towing to Bermuda
The tow to Bermuda was a logistical and technical challenge, as the U-505 was damaged, partially flooded, and not under its own power. Here’s how it was managed:

1. **Initial Tow by USS Pillsbury**:
   - The USS Pillsbury took the U-505 in tow immediately after the boarding party secured it. However, the Pillsbury’s tow cable became entangled in the U-505’s diving planes, damaging the destroyer’s hull and forcing it to withdraw due to flooding.
   - The USS Guadalcanal then took over, attaching a towline to the U-505. The carrier’s crew, with assistance from the boarding party, pumped water out of the submarine to keep it buoyant.

2. **Journey to Bermuda**:
   - The task group towed the U-505 approximately 1,700 miles to **Bermuda**, arriving on June 19, 1944. The journey took about two weeks, as towing a damaged submarine across the Atlantic was slow and required constant monitoring to prevent sinking.
   - The U-505 was kept operational by U.S. Navy personnel who manned its pumps and maintained its stability. The submarine’s diesel engines were inoperable due to damage, so the tow relied entirely on the Guadalcanal’s power.
   - To maintain secrecy, the task group avoided contact with other ships and kept radio silence. The capture was a closely guarded secret to prevent the Germans from realizing their Enigma codes and other secrets had been compromised.

3. **Secrecy and Handling**:
   - Upon arrival in Bermuda, the U-505 was hidden at a remote anchorage in **Port Royal Bay**. The German crew was interned in a secret camp, isolated from other prisoners to maintain the illusion that the U-505 had sunk.
   - U.S. Navy intelligence teams thoroughly examined the U-505, recovering additional documents, charts, and equipment. The captured Enigma machine and codebooks were sent to Bletchley Park, enhancing Allied codebreaking efforts, though the Ultra program was already advanced by 1944.
   - The U-505 was repaired and tested by the U.S. Navy in Bermuda to study its technology, including its acoustic torpedoes and sonar countermeasures.

### Significance and Aftermath
- **Intelligence Value**: The capture of U-505 provided the Allies with a wealth of intelligence, including up-to-date Enigma settings, operational orders, and insights into U-boat tactics and technology. While the Enigma materials were not as critical as earlier captures (like U-110 in 1941), they helped confirm and refine Allied codebreaking efforts.
- **Morale and Propaganda**: The capture was a morale boost for the U.S. Navy and a psychological blow to the Kriegsmarine. It demonstrated the Allies’ growing dominance in the Atlantic.
- **Post-War Fate**: After the war, the U-505 was nearly scrapped, but Captain Gallery, a Chicago native, advocated for its preservation. In 1954, it was donated to the **Museum of Science and Industry in Chicago**, where it was towed via the St. Lawrence Seaway and Great Lakes. It remains a centerpiece exhibit, restored to near-original condition, with tours showcasing its interior and the story of its capture.

- **Recognition**: Lieutenant Albert David received the **Medal of Honor** for leading the boarding party, the only such award for a boarding action in World War II. Two other members of the boarding party, **Zenon B. Lukosius** and **Wayne M. Pickels**, received the Navy Cross, and others were awarded Silver Stars. Captain Gallery received the Distinguished Service Medal for his leadership.

### Key Facts About U-505’s Capture and Tow
- **Date of Capture**: June 4, 1944
- **Location**: Approximately 150 miles west of Cape Blanco, French West Africa (coordinates roughly 21°30’N, 19°20’W)
- **Task Group**: USS Guadalcanal (CVE-60), USS Pillsbury (DE-133), USS Pope (DE-134), USS Flaherty (DE-135), USS Chatelain (DE-149), USS Jenks (DE-665)
- **Tow Distance**: ~1,700 miles to Bermuda
- **Arrival in Bermuda**: June 19, 1944
- **Crew**: 58 German prisoners taken; 1 fatality (Hans Goebeler)
- **Current Location**: Museum of Science and Industry, Chicago, since 1954

### Why It Matters
The capture of U-505 was a rare and daring feat, marking the first time the U.S. Navy captured an enemy warship at sea since the 19th century. The operation required split-second decisions, bravery under fire, and engineering ingenuity to keep the submarine afloat. The tow to Bermuda, while less dramatic than the capture, was a testament to the Navy’s ability to adapt and preserve a valuable prize under challenging conditions. Today, the U-505 stands as a tangible link to the Battle of the Atlantic, preserving the legacy of those who risked their lives to turn the tide against the U-boat threat.

### Background on U-110
- **Construction**: The U-110 was built at the **AG Weser** shipyard in **Bremen, Germany**, launched on August 25, 1940, and commissioned into the Kriegsmarine on November 21, 1940. It was 76.5 meters (251 feet) long, slightly shorter than the later Type IXC U-505, and designed for long-range operations.
- **Service**: Under the command of **Kapitänleutnant Fritz-Julius Lemp**, a skilled but controversial U-boat commander, the U-110 had completed two patrols before its capture, sinking 10 Allied ships totaling about 45,000 tons. Lemp was infamous for sinking the passenger liner **Athenia** in 1939 (on U-30), an incident that strained German neutrality claims early in the war.
- **Context in 1941**: The Battle of the Atlantic was at a critical stage. German U-boats were devastating Allied convoys, and the Allies were struggling to decrypt the Kriegsmarine’s Enigma communications, which used a more complex version of the machine than the German Army or Air Force. Capturing a naval Enigma and its codebooks was a top priority for Allied intelligence.

### The Capture: May 9, 1941
The U-110 was captured during an attack on **Convoy OB-318**, a westbound convoy of 38 merchant ships sailing from Liverpool to North America, escorted by ships of the **Royal Navy’s 3rd Escort Group**, commanded by **Commander Addison Joe Baker-Cresswell** aboard the destroyer **HMS Bulldog**. The capture took place south of Iceland, in the North Atlantic. Here’s how it unfolded:

1. **Detection and Attack**:
   - On May 9, 1941, the U-110, part of a wolfpack, was stalking Convoy OB-318. At around 10:30 AM, the corvette **HMS Aubrietia** detected the U-110 via **ASDIC** (sonar) while it was submerged. The U-110 had just torpedoed two merchant ships, the **Esmond** and **Bengore Head**, alerting the escorts to its presence.
   - HMS Aubrietia launched a pattern of **depth charges**, which severely damaged the U-110. The explosions disabled the submarine’s hydroplanes, ruptured fuel tanks, and caused flooding, forcing Lemp to order the boat to surface to avoid sinking.

2. **Surfacing and Abandonment**:
   - At approximately 11:00 AM, the U-110 surfaced near the convoy, about 100 miles south of Iceland (near coordinates 60°N, 33°W). The escorts, including HMS Bulldog, HMS Broadway (another destroyer), and HMS Aubrietia, opened fire with machine guns and light weapons to prevent the German crew from manning the deck gun or scuttling the submarine.
   - Lemp ordered his crew to abandon ship, believing the U-110 was sinking. The Germans evacuated into the water, expecting scuttling charges to destroy the boat. However, the charges were either not set properly or failed to detonate, leaving the U-110 intact.

3. **Boarding Operation**:
   - Recognizing the opportunity, Commander Baker-Cresswell on HMS Bulldog organized a boarding party led by **Sub-Lieutenant David Balme**, a 20-year-old officer. Balme and a small team rowed to the U-110 in a whaler, boarding the submarine under the risk of it sinking or exploding.
   - The boarding party found the U-110 deserted but operational, with minimal flooding. They quickly secured the boat and began collecting materials. Balme himself entered the radio room and retrieved an **Enigma machine** (a four-rotor naval model), along with **codebooks**, **cipher keys**, and other documents critical to German naval communications. These included the **Kurzsignalheft** (short-signal codebook) and **Kenngruppenbuch** (indicator group book), used for encrypting U-boat messages.
   - The team also gathered charts, logs, and other intelligence materials. They worked rapidly, as the submarine was still at risk of sinking or being scuttled if any remaining charges detonated.

4. **Securing the U-110**:
   - The boarding party ensured the U-110 remained afloat by closing valves and securing hatches. HMS Bulldog attached a towline to the U-110, intending to bring it to a British port for further study.
   - Of the U-110’s crew, 32 were rescued as prisoners, but 15, including Commander Lemp, perished. Lemp’s fate is debated: some accounts suggest he was shot while attempting to reboard the U-110 to scuttle it, while others, including British records, indicate he drowned, possibly deliberately, to avoid capture.

### Towing Attempt and Sinking
- **Towing to Iceland**: HMS Bulldog began towing the U-110 toward Iceland, specifically **Hvalfjord**, a British naval base. The tow began on May 9, 1941, with the goal of delivering the submarine for detailed inspection. The U-110 was damaged but stable enough to remain afloat initially, and the tow proceeded under escort to maintain secrecy.
- **Sinking**: On May 10, 1941, while under tow, the U-110 began taking on water, likely due to worsening damage from the depth charges or leaks that could not be fully contained. The submarine became unstable and sank stern-first before reaching Iceland, disappearing into the Atlantic. The exact location of the sinking is not precisely documented but was roughly in the same area south of Iceland.
- **Outcome of Sinking**: Unlike the U-505, which was successfully towed to Bermuda and preserved, the U-110 was lost to the sea. However, the critical intelligence materials had already been removed by Balme’s team, ensuring the operation’s primary objective was achieved.

### Significance and Impact
- **Intelligence Haul**: The capture of the U-110’s Enigma machine and codebooks was a turning point in the Allied codebreaking effort at **Bletchley Park**. The materials allowed cryptanalysts, including **Alan Turing** and his team, to break the Kriegsmarine’s **Hydra cipher** (later called “Dolphin” by the Allies), used for U-boat communications. This enabled the Allies to read U-boat signals with greater speed and accuracy, often in near real-time, by late 1941.
  - The codebooks provided settings for the naval Enigma, which used additional rotors and more complex wiring than the Army or Luftwaffe versions, making it harder to crack without captured materials.
  - The intelligence helped the Allies reroute convoys, avoid U-boat wolfpacks, and target submarines more effectively, saving countless ships and lives.
- **Secrecy**: The capture was kept secret to prevent the Germans from suspecting their codes were compromised. The German crew was isolated, and the Kriegsmarine believed the U-110 had sunk with all hands and materials lost. This secrecy allowed the Allies to exploit the intelligence for months without German countermeasures.
- **Strategic Impact**: The U-110’s materials were instrumental in turning the tide of the Battle of the Atlantic. By 1942, Allied losses to U-boats decreased, and U-boat sinkings increased, partly due to improved intelligence. The capture complemented other codebreaking successes and laid the groundwork for later captures, like the U-505 in 1944.
- **Recognition**: Sub-Lieutenant David Balme was awarded the **Distinguished Service Cross** for his bravery and leadership. Commander Baker-Cresswell and other escort group members also received commendations for their roles.
